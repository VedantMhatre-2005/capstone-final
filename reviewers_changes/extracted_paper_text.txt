A quantum-enhanced graph learning framework for edge-level
traffic forecasting and QAOA-based congestion rerouting
Dr. Sharmila Senguptaa, Vedant Mhatrea, Aditya Upasania, Yash Mahajana, Sanjay Askia
aDepartment of Computer Engineering, Vivekanand Education Society’s Institute of Technology, Mumbai 400074, India
Abstract
Urban traffic congestion results from complicated spatiotemporal interactions throughout connected road networks. As such, both
short-term forecasting and network-wide rerouting are essential for responsive traffic management. This paper proposes a quantum-
enhanced framework that integrates edge-level traffic forecasting with combinatorial rerouting optimization. A Spatiotemporal
Quantum Graph Convolutional Network (STQGCN) predicts short-term congestion on road segments by combining classical graph
message passing with a Variational Quantum Circuit (VQC) layer that enhances learned representations through parameterized
rotations and entanglement. The congested edges are identified, and the predicted congestion states are discretized into packets and
candidate detours are formulated as a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) problem, which is solved by using a
depth-one Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) with classical parameter tuning. Additionally, the framework
is evaluated against Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and classical Spatiotemporal Graph Convolutional Network (STGCN)
forecasting baselines and a Binary Particle Swarm Optimization (BPSO) rerouting baseline, across single and hierarchical network
topologies and 45 heterogeneous traffic scenarios. The STQGCN achieves competitive or superior forecasting accuracy, especially
on bidirectional flow topologies, while using four orders of magnitude fewer trainable parameters than the LSTM baseline. QAOA-
based rerouting achieves median peak-occupancy reductions of 13–22% depending on network size and never produces a higher
final peak occupancy than BPSO across all evaluated scenarios. The results demonstrate the feasibility of pairing quantum-enhanced
representation learning with quantum combinatorial optimization within a unified traffic management pipeline.
Keywords: Quantum Machine Learning; Spatiotemporal Graph Neural Networks; Traffic Congestion Forecasting; QAOA; QUBO; Intelligent
Transportation Systems; Traffic Optimization;
1. Introduction
Continuous real-time data generation is a prominent feature of modern urban mobility and transportation systems.
In order to produce dense traffic streams with a high frequency at the level of individual road segments, Intelligent
Transportation Systems (ITS) and Connected and Automated Vehicles (CAVs) increasingly utilize heterogeneous
sensor modalities, like loop detectors, roadside cameras, radar and connected-vehicle telemetry [8, 2]. Due to this
shift from sparse and periodically sampled measurements toward continuous edge-level observation, it is possible
for systems to act on localized, short-horizon signals as they are generated rather than reacting to network-wide

and decision-making methods that operate at the same edge-level details as the underlying data, thereby motivating

Rapid urbanization and rising mobility demand have put substantial pressure on urban transportation networks,
causing traffic congestion that affects travel efficiency, road utilization and the reliability of daily commute. While ITS
and CAVs are capable of generating continuous traffic information from distributed transportation infrastructure to
allow more responsive and data-driven traffic management [8, 2], traffic conditions remain inherently spatiotemporal,
which means the state of one road segment is affected by neighboring junctions, road capacity, signal conditions,
upstream and downstream traffic, as well as evolving demand over time. Since junctions and road segments form an
interconnected topology in which information flows between neighboring components, graph-based learning provides
a natural representation for such systems. Spatiotemporal graph convolutional architectures have demonstrated the
ability to learn such structured and temporal relationships, providing a foundation for modeling complex networked
systems [12].
Recent research has explored quantum-enhanced learning for transportation applications, including quantum ma-
chine learning for traffic-density estimation [9]. However, curbing congestion requires converting predicted traffic
states into actionable network-level decisions. Classical optimization techniques, such as PSO, have been widely
studied for traffic signal timing and congestion mitigation due to their adaptability and low implementation complex-
ity [3, 6, 7], but swarm-based search can suffer from parameter sensitivity, premature convergence and increasing
computational effort as the decision space and network size grow. Quantum computing has emerged as a promis-
ing alternative in this context, with prior work demonstrating quantum-assisted traffic navigation and quantum-based
traffic-flow optimization [1, 11], thereby motivating further discussion into combining quantum-enhanced prediction
with quantum combinatorial optimization for network-level traffic management. Since each congested edge can be
independently routed along its original path or a candidate detour, the space of rerouting decisions expands and grows
with the number of congested links, and classical metaheuristic algorithms must search this space heuristically without
taking advantage of its structure. In contrast, quantum optimization algorithms encode the decision space directly onto
qubits and offers a different approach to this combinatorial explosion, a property which is revisited when examining
scalability in Section 6.2.7.
Adding to these developments, this paper proposes a quantum-enhanced traffic management framework that in-
tegrates an STQGCN for short-term edge-level traffic prediction with a QUBO formulation solved using QAOA for
network-level rerouting, thereby combining prediction and optimization within a single closed-loop pipeline [4, 10, 5].
The main contributions of this work are:
1. A hybrid classical-quantum STQGCN architecture, that combines graph-based message passing with a VQC
layer to produce short-term, edge-level predictions of traffic congestion directly on road segments, has been
proposed.
2. Network-level traffic rerouting has been formulated as a QUBO problem, which has been solved using a depth-
one QAOA, thus establishing a closed prediction-to-optimization loop in which the predicted congestion directly
informs rerouting and signal-retuning decisions rather than treating prediction and optimization as independent
stages.
3. The proposed framework was evaluated against LSTM and classical STGCN forecasting baselines and a BPSO
rerouting baseline, across single and hierarchical network topologies and 45 heterogeneous traffic scenarios.
Furthermore, the framework has been established within a corridor-level system architecture and Defense-in-
Depth security model compatible with existing ATCS infrastructure.
2. Literature Survey
2.1. Classical Traffic Optimization
Traditionally, intelligent traffic management systems have relied on classical optimization and classical learning
techniques to mitigate congestion, coordinate traffic signals and reroute vehicles. Although PSO and the other swarm-

to premature convergence and reduced solution quality.
2.2. Graph-Based Traffic Prediction
Additionally, graph-based deep learning has proven to be effective in capturing structured traffic dependencies.
Spatiotemporal graph convolutional architectures learn relationships over interconnected nodes and evolving states
[12], while quantum-enhanced studies are beneficial for traffic-density estimation [9, 8].
2.3. Quantum Computing in Transportation
Quantum computing has been considered a promising technology to address challenges in transportation opti-
mization, as routing, signal coordination and redistribution are combinatorial problems. Early works have shown that
quantum assisted traffic finding and flow optimization is feasible through quantum annealing [1, 11] and recent works
have formulated this as a QUBO problem for quantum optimization [4, 10]. Quantum circuits offer an alternative
through QAOA for the near-term hybrid quantum-classical era [5] and works have explored this approach alongside
other edge quantum architectures and applications for general transportation optimization [2, 8]. However, the quan-
tum advantage in such works remains limited by circuit depth and hardware availability and practical implementations
would utilize a hybrid approach that combines classical and quantum processing.
2.4. Research Gap
While previous works have focused on either learning methods for prediction or defining optimization problems,
practical implementation considering both aspects for traffic congestion prediction and coordinated optimization with
rerouting decisions is not prevalent. This gap can be observed even among the quantum computing works discussed
above. For instance, Yarkoni et al. [1] and Salloum et al. [10] propose traffic navigation and signal-flow rerouting
as quantum annealing or iterative QUBO problems, but in both cases, the traffic states causing the optimization are
an externally supplied input rather than being produced by a forecasting model. In contrast, Wang et al. [9] apply
quantum machine learning to estimate traffic density but do not optimize the resulting predictions. Therefore, neither
line of work establishes a pipeline in which predicted congestion states directly inform network-level optimization.
For practical deployment, continuous congestion prediction, using a learned model, is required to inform large-scale
coordinated optimization while being compatible with current ITS infrastructure.
This work proposes a hybrid classical-quantum framework for coordinated optimization based on spatiotemporal
graph learning and quantum optimization. The proposed solution employs graph message passing with a VQC layer
for short-term traffic congestion forecast at the edge level. The predicted congestion is then used as an input to a QUBO
formulation solved with QAOA to determine optimal rerouting decisions for an adaptive and responsive smart city
transport system. This combined approach differs from previous works which have treated the congestion prediction
and optimization aspects in isolation.
3. Proposed Methodology
3.1. Traffic Congestion Prediction
As observed in Figure 1, an STQGCN is proposed for edge-level traffic prediction by modeling junction spatial
dependencies, segment physical attributes, dynamic weather conditions and temporal periodicity.

4. System Implementation and Security
The proposed solution is designed as a corridor-based, hybrid centralized-hierarchical traffic-control system that
will improve the existing Adaptive Traffic Control System (ATCS). The field includes AI cameras, radar, weather,
signal heads, VMS, an MMU and the certified controller cabinet for every junction, where the existing controller will
maintain final control over signal operations after considering the data on vehicle traffic, queue and occupancy, as
well as the weather, provided by the field equipment [13, 14, 15]. The system will be incorporated into Mumbai’s
hierarchical traffic-control system. A managed fibre/MLLN will be used, a Ward Edge Platform will carry out the
optimization process and the city traffic management centre will have overall supervision [13, 14, 15, 16]. Every
corridor is represented as a graph, with junctions as nodes and segments as edges describing the topology, and the
STQGCN model processes this graph’s local state, learning the patterns of spillback and platoon propagation and
optimizing offsets, cycle length, phase, priority and VMS policy.
The operation follows: Ward Edge aggregates observations into a corridor graph, the STQGCN predicts short-
term traffic and QAOA is invoked only when adjustment is beneficial (event-driven optimization). QAOA currently
runs on a classical simulator, decoupled from but compatible with future QPU deployment. Where central quantum
connectivity is needed, a 4G/5G private APN or a WPC-approved 5.825–5.875 GHz wireless bridge, targeting ∼16–
20 Mbps per junction and sub-50 ms latency can be specified, though Ward Edge remains the primary low-latency
layer [17, 18]. The execution is conservative: plans pass through the Implementation Decision Engine (IDE), Oper-
ational Decision Engine (ODE), safety validation and a Signal Transition Manager before reaching the controller;

5. Experimental Setup and Datasets
5.1. Dataset Formulation and Realistic Traffic Synthesis
Standard benchmarks (e.g., PeMSD4/D8) often lack spatial anisotropy, dynamic edge-level attributes and struc-
tural heterogeneity. To evaluate STQGCNs under realistic urban dynamics, two physics-informed synthetic datasets
modeled on the Mumbai metropolitan transportation network were constructed:
1. Single Network (N = 8): Localized junction hub with 8 signalized intersections connected by 11 unidirectional
and 22 bidirectional directed links.
2. Hierarchical Network (N = 24): Multi-zonal corridor split into three 8-node operational zones, joined by inter-
zone bridges (V8 →V9 and V16 →V17) with 30 unidirectional and 60 bidirectional directed links:
• Zone 1 (Tree Topology): Suburban inflow feeder that lets commuters flow into central trunks.
• Zone 2 (Mesh Topology, 2 × 4 grid): Central Business District (CBD) interchange experiencing multidirec-
tional weaving flow.
• Zone 3 (Linear Topology): South Mumbai narrow coastal corridor acting as a capacity bottleneck.
Both datasets span NT = 10, 000 5-second timesteps capturing diurnal/weekend cycles and 5-second lookahead
targets. Node feature matrices X ∈RNT ×N×6 encode vehicle volume (veh/hr), mean speed (km/h), density (veh/km),
monsoon rain intensity (mm/hr), incident flags (p = 0.03) and signal split ratios. Edge feature matrices E ∈RNT ×|E|×6
track link capacity (veh/hr), length (km), PCI, speed limit (km/h), lane count (3–4) and congestion ratio (q/C).

6. Results and Discussion
6.1. Experimental Evaluation for Short-Term Traffic Prediction
The STQGCN prediction phase is evaluated over a 5-second horizon against two baselines: an LSTM (non-spatial
sequence baseline) and a classical STGCN (lacking the VQC update layer). Benchmark topologies include:
1. Single Network (N = 8): Localized junction topology with 11 unidirectional / 22 bidirectional links.
2. Hierarchical Network (N = 24): 3-zone arterial system with 30 unidirectional / 60 bidirectional links.
Performance is reported on un-normalized throughput (veh/hr) using MAE, RMSE, MAPE (%) and R2.
6.1.1. Architectural Performance Comparison

1. Bidirectional Resilience: In turbulent bidirectional flows, quantum entanglement enables STQGCN (K = 6) to
outperform classical baselines, achieving the lowest MAE (238.40 veh/hr) and MAPE (24.67%) on the single
network, as well as the lowest MAPE (18.50%) on the hierarchical Network.
2. Scale Generalization: Graph-structured models outperform sequence-only LSTM on the 24-node topology
(4.22% vs 4.70% MAPE unidirectional). STQGCN maintains tight error bounds (MAE = 46.68 veh/hr,
R2 = 0.9905), proving scalable integration of quantum graph updates across zonal boundaries.
3. STQGCN vs. Classical STGCN: Though the STQGCN showcases strong spatiotemporal representation ca-
pabilities, the analysis indicates that it outperforms classical STGCN only selectively under specific network
conditions rather than uniformly across all traffic scenarios. Specifically, the STQGCN performs better in com-
plex, bidirectional flow topologies (for example, reducing MAE to 238.40 veh/hr on the single bidirectional
network and 175.27 veh/hr on the hierarchical bidirectional network) as well as localized networks with a high
density. In these complex scenarios, non-linear cross-edge spatiotemporal correlations are created by the counter-
directional vehicle interactions and shockwaves produced by bottlenecks. The VQC effectively maps these com-
plex interactions by leveraging RY(θ) state rotations and CNOT entangling gates to construct high-dimensional
Hilbert space representations that exceed the expressive capacity of classical linear graph projections. In con-
trast, in smooth and highly ordered unidirectional topologies (like the hierarchical unidirectional network, where
R2 > 0.99), spatiotemporal flow propagation is mainly deterministic and linear spatial message passing in clas-
sical STGCN is already near-optimal. Under such low-variance conditions, quantum entanglement is beneficial
only slightly, while non-linear quantum measurement projections can introduce minor parameter optimization
variance, thereby allowing classical STGCN to perform better than the STQGCN (for example, 46.27 veh/hr vs.
46.68 veh/hr MAE).
6.1.2. Quantum Advantage and Expressivity Analysis
Ablation across VQC depths K ∈{1, . . . , 7} under unidirectional flow showcases scale-dependent optimization
dynamics: on the Single Network (N = 8) (Fig. 2a), the maximum accuracy is observed at depth K = 1 (MAE =
152.87 veh/hr, MAPE = 14.45%), with K = 2 causing significant error inflation (MAE = 162.24 veh/hr) from local
parameter redundancy, while deeper configurations (K ≥6) show slight variance rise (MAE ≈161.17–162.25 veh/hr).
On the Hierarchical Network (N = 24) (Fig. 2b)), error decreases steadily from K = 1 (MAE = 48.27 veh/hr) to an
optimal minimum at K = 4 (MAE = 46.68 veh/hr, MAPE = 4.22%, R2 = 0.9905), beyond which performance
plateaus and degrades slightly (MAE = 48.00 veh/hr at K = 7).
These results highlight three algorithmic advantages of STQGCN:
1. Parameter Efficiency: While LSTM uses 386, 974 trainable parameters, STQGCN appends only 9 × K varia-
tional parameters (Θ ∈R36 at K = 4). Despite four orders of magnitude fewer parameters, STQGCN delivers
superior accuracy under complex bidirectional flow.
2. Hilbert Space Entanglement: Embedding features via RY(θ) rotations and CNOT entangling gates constructs
high-dimensional feature maps that model non-linear bottleneck shockwaves better than linear graph projections.
3. Barren Plateau Mitigation at K = 4: Medium depths (K ∈[1, 4]) expand expressive capacity without gradient
decay, whereas deeper circuits (K ≥6) risk quantum barren plateaus. Depth K = 4 optimizes expressivity while
preserving robust parameter-shift gradients.
6.2. Comparative Analysis: BPSO vs. QAOA Optimization
The proposed traffic optimization framework based on QAOA has been benchmarked across 45 heterogeneous

6.2.3. Algorithm Execution Latency
As shown in Fig. 3, the execution latency of BPSO and QAOA increases with network size. BPSO achieves low
algorithm execution runtimes of 8.2 ms, 14.5 ms and 24.8 ms across the 4-node, 5-node and 6-node topologies re-
spectively, thereby benefiting from the low complexity of the vector arithmetic during particle velocity and posi-
tion updates. However, QAOA introduces a slight computational overhead due to variational angle (γ, β) evaluation
and statevector simulation and yields latencies of 18.4 ms, 26.2 ms and 42.1 ms across the 4-node, 5-node and 6-
node topologies respectively. Despite this higher baseline overhead, QAOA maintains sub-50 ms real-time execution
bounds, confirming that its quantum optimization approach scales efficiently for dynamic traffic control without suf-
fering from latency bottlenecks.

6.2.5. QUBO Hyperparameter Sensitivity Analysis & Sensitivity Ablation
In order to address potential sensitivity to fixed parameter tuning, an ablation was performed over key QUBO
formulation hyperparameters: the congestion detection threshold θhigh ∈[0.50, 0.70], target load occupancy δ ∈
[0.40, 0.55] and maximum packet limit Ncap ∈[4, 12]. Varying θhigh across 0.50–0.70 demonstrated near-invariant
peak congestion reduction (18.94% ± 6.66% across representative scenarios) and stable active variable counts
(N = 6.2 qubits), proving that the congestion detection heuristic is robust to slight changes in the threshold value
and does not rely on hyperparameter tuning. Varying the target occupancy δ showed a smooth, predictable trade-off
between rerouted traffic volume and QUBO penalty terms, with mean objective cost decreasing from 0.72 at δ = 0.40
to 0.21 at δ = 0.55 while maintaining peak reductions above 13.7%–23.0%. Finally, increasing the packet capacity
limit Ncap from 4 to 12 confirmed performance saturation at Ncap ≥10, where average active qubit allocation stabilizes
at N = 6.2 qubits with mean peak reduction saturating at 18.94%.
6.2.6. Quantum Circuit Scalability
The QAOA circuit width, gate depth and wall-clock execution time were benchmarked against BPSO across the
4-Node, 5-Node and 6-Node mesh topologies. QAOA binary variable requirements scaled from an average of N =
7.6 qubits on the 4-Node network to N = 7.8 qubits on the 6-Node networks. Circuit depth scaled linearly with ansatz
depth p, measuring d = 56–58 for p = 1 (27–28 two-qubit CNOT/RZZ gates) and d = 167–173 for p = 3. In terms
of wall-clock execution, QAOA parameter optimization via classical COBYLA averaged 4.24–5.93 ms per scenario,
outperforming BPSO wall-clock execution time (15.78–20.75 ms for M = 30 particles across T = 20 iterations,
totaling 600 objective evaluations).

scenarios at p = 3. Hence, these metrics validate that the QUBO formulation caps quantum circuit requirements within

6.2.7. Scalability and Prospects for Quantum Advantage
The QUBO formulation has a solution space of size 2N, where N is the number of binary decision variables.
While classical metaheuristic algorithms, such as PSO, do not perform an exhaustive search, their costs and solution

13
quality may degrade with an increasing N. QAOA encodes the decision variables in N qubits and a 2N-dimensional
quantum state and is therefore efficient in large-scale optimization. Recent works report favorable scaling properties
for particular QAOA variants on combinatorial optimization problems. These works include numerical results on
quantum processors with up to 109 qubits, but they do not establish quantum advantage for any optimization problem.
To assess the practical relevance of the proposed traffic optimization framework, further investigation on the solution
quality, time-to-solution and resource requirements at higher N is required to establish a favorable scaling behavior.
7. Conclusion
7.1. Summary of Findings
This paper presented a unified quantum-enhanced traffic management framework that pairs an STQGCN for short-
term edge-level congestion forecasting with a QUBO-QAOA formulation for network-level rerouting. Across single
and hierarchical Mumbai-modeled network topologies, STQGCN matched or exceeded LSTM and classical STGCN
prediction accuracy, especially under bidirectional flow, while using fewer trainable parameters. The QAOA-based
rerouting achieved median peak-occupancy reductions of 13–22% depending on network size and never produced
a higher final peak occupancy than the BPSO baseline across all 45 distinct scenarios, thereby demonstrating that
near-term, depth-one QAOA circuits can perform as well as or better than a classical metaheuristic algorithm on a
combinatorial traffic problem. Hence, the results suggest the feasibility of combining the two phases, while the ac-
companying corridor-level system architecture and Defense-in-Depth security model suggest a deployment approach
that is compatible with the existing ATCS infrastructure.
7.2. Limitations
Addressing the limitations of the proposed framework, the evaluation relies on physics-informed synthetic datasets
rather than live sensor feeds, and real-world noise, which may affect forecasting accuracy, has been neglected. Fur-
thermore, the QUBO formulation is restricted to at most 10 binary decision variables per scenario for computational
feasibility. Since QAOA was run on a classical simulator at depth one rather than physical quantum hardware, neither
its scalability to more complex congestion events nor genuine quantum advantage has been established.
7.3. Future Scope
Future work will extend the framework by scaling the QUBO/QAOA formulation and evaluating on actual QPU
hardware at larger N, validating the STQGCN and the rerouting pipeline against live field data from deployed Mumbai
ATCS infrastructure, as well as ingesting and processing live CCTV video streams directly using computer vision to
derive vehicle counts, queue lengths and incident flags in real time.
